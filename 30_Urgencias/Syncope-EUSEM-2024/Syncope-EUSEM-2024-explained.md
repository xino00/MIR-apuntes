# Syncope in the ED — the EUSEM 2024 pathway explained

[[30_Urgencias/Syncope-EUSEM-2024/Syncope-EUSEM-2024-pathway.canvas|Open the interactive Canvas]]

> [!abstract] The question that holds the pathway together
> The blackout may have ended before the patient reaches you. Your task is to establish what happened, identify any illness requiring immediate treatment, and decide how safely the remaining uncertainty can be investigated.

This is a learning companion to Möckel et al., *The syncope core management process in the emergency department: a consensus statement of the EUSEM syncope group* (2024). The population is patients presenting to an emergency department with suspected syncope or transient loss of consciousness (TLOC). The Canvas is an original educational redraw of the clinical logic, not a reproduction of the published figure or a locally approved protocol. Source [1] supplies the main pathway; explicitly labelled **ESC context** adds a short clarification about disposition from source [2]. Prepared 27 October 2026; this is an explanation of the 2024 paper rather than a comprehensive review of all later syncope evidence.

## How to read the Canvas

Read from top to bottom. Blue cards describe assessment; yellow cards pose decisions; red cards indicate an urgent or disease-specific pathway; green marks the low-risk destination. Purple cards contain interpretation or source notes. Colour supports the written labels and is not a numerical risk score.

The pathway has three main phases: **arrival and triage**, **diagnostic assessment**, and **risk-based disposition**. Side branches are meaningful exits: when another illness explains the presentation, care continues under that illness's pathway. They do not mean that assessment or treatment stops.

Each numbered card links to the relevant explanation below. Related existing notes are listed at the end; they have not been revised as part of this explainer.

## 1 — Start with the event

“The patient fainted” is often the first description, but it is already an interpretation. Start with what can actually be established: an apparent transient loss of consciousness, its circumstances and its recovery. Syncope is the subset caused by transient global cerebral hypoperfusion. The distinction keeps the differential diagnosis open long enough to recognise a seizure, psychogenic TLOC, primary head injury or a more persistent disturbance of consciousness. [1, Introduction; pp. 250–251]

The mechanism is important because similar outward appearances can arise from very different illnesses. Rapid recovery is compatible with syncope, but no isolated detail should carry the whole diagnosis. The account from a witness may be more useful than the patient's account of the period they cannot remember.

The paper also brings near-syncope into the assessment process. It describes this as partial TLOC; strictly, presyncope does not include complete loss of consciousness. The practical point is to assess its possible cause carefully, rather than dismiss it because the patient did not fully black out. [1, part 1; p. 253]

## 2 — Separate trauma and shock early

The first phase is a safety screen. In the paper's process model, triage is followed by identifying a traumatic cause of TLOC and assessing for shock. In real care, recognition and treatment of instability take priority and can happen simultaneously; nobody should wait for the next box on a diagram before resuscitating an unstable patient. This is a practical interpretation of the process model. [1, part 1; p. 253]

### Did trauma cause the blackout, or follow it?

These are different questions. If head trauma caused the loss of consciousness, the patient belongs in a traumatic-TLOC pathway. If syncope caused a fall, both the injury and the reason for the collapse require attention. Finding an injury does not settle the original cause. The authors particularly emphasise the difficulty in older people, people with dementia and unwitnessed falls. [1, Discussion; p. 257]

### Is the patient in shock?

Shock is an immediate problem in its own right. The paper highlights sepsis, but the action is cause-directed shock care, not an assumption that all shock is septic. The syncope work-up must not distract from physiological instability. After these urgent branches have been addressed, the working description is still simply **nontraumatic TLOC without shock**; syncope has not yet been established. [1, part 1; p. 253]

## 3 — Establish the initial clinical picture

The next phase has parallel components: vital signs, an ECG and laboratory testing when appropriate. History and examination can begin immediately and are completed alongside these results. The paper is organising responsibilities and sequence, not proposing that clinicians wait for blood results before taking a history. [1, part 2; pp. 253, 256]

### Vital signs and examination

Record blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate and temperature, including what was already measured at triage. Document the findings and examine for clues that would change the pathway: cardiac disease, neurological abnormalities, injury or an acute systemic illness. A reassuring set of observations describes the patient now; it does not by itself reconstruct the earlier event. [1, Table 1; p. 256; final sentence is clinical interpretation]

### ECG: acquisition is only half the task

The consensus calls for qualified interpretation and documentation **within 10 minutes of registration**. This is an operational target proposed by the group. It assigns responsibility for recognising a dangerous trace promptly; obtaining an ECG without timely review does not achieve that goal. [1, part 2 and Table 3; pp. 253, 256]

Selected ECG findings in the paper that warrant particular attention include:

- Acute ischaemic changes.
- Mobitz II or third-degree atrioventricular block.
- Marked awake bradycardia, slow atrial fibrillation or significant pauses, interpreted in their physiological context.
- Ventricular tachycardia.
- Pacemaker or ICD malfunction.
- A type 1 Brugada pattern or repeatedly prolonged QTc.
- Conduction abnormalities or changes suggesting structural heart disease.

These are examples from Table 3, not a complete diagnostic checklist. The table also lists less specific findings whose importance depends on a history compatible with arrhythmic syncope. An abnormal ECG may be a risk marker without proving the cause of the event. A normal short recording cannot exclude an intermittent arrhythmia; that is a clinical limitation of the test, not evidence that the episode was benign. [1, Table 3; p. 256; last two sentences are clinical interpretation]

### Blood tests: a hypothesis determines the panel

Laboratory practice was one of the points on which the European experts initially differed. Table 2 specifies glucose and haemoglobin as the minimum tests **if recommended in the particular setting**. The narrative is more categorical, but the table's qualification matters: the article does not justify an indiscriminate comprehensive panel for every episode. [1, Methods and Table 2; pp. 252, 256]

The authors list additional tests at the clinician's discretion and others tied to a suspected diagnosis, including cardiac biomarkers for suspected infarction and D-dimer in appropriate investigation of thromboembolic or aortic disease. The presence of a test in this table is not an instruction to order it routinely. A positive D-dimer does not establish pulmonary embolism, and a negative troponin does not establish a benign cause of syncope. Those last two statements explain why tests must answer a clinical question. [1, Table 2; p. 256; interpretation explicitly noted]

## 4 — Reconstruct the episode and consider orthostasis

Use the history to assemble a timeline. This is a practical way to apply the paper's requirement for history and examination, rather than a separate validated prediction tool. [1, part 2; p. 253]

| Moment | What you are trying to establish |
| --- | --- |
| Before | Posture, activity, trigger, warning symptoms, palpitations, pain or breathlessness. |
| During | Witnessed loss of awareness, apparent duration, injury, movements and their sequence. |
| After | Speed and completeness of recovery; persistent confusion, symptoms or focal abnormalities. |
| Background | Similar episodes, cardiac disease, medication exposure and relevant family history. |

The paper recommends considering a Schellong/active-standing assessment when suitable. It helps test whether a postural blood-pressure change plausibly explains the event. The interpretation must fit the history and symptoms; measuring a fall in blood pressure does not automatically exclude another cause. This guide does not add an orthostatic threshold or a full autonomic-testing protocol, because neither is developed in the consensus paper. [1, part 2; p. 253; interpretation explicitly noted]

Persistent altered consciousness or an account that remains inconsistent with syncope should lead back to the differential diagnosis. “Unexplained syncope” and “we do not yet know whether this was syncope” are different clinical states.

## 5 — Decide which problem you are treating

After the initial evaluation, the paper separates three principal situations. A fourth route in this educational diagram makes its recurring requirement to reconsider non-syncopal TLOC explicit. [1, parts 2–3; pp. 256–257]

### A — Syncope is a symptom of another illness

Examples in the article include pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection and sepsis without shock. That illness now determines the diagnostic and treatment pathway. A generic syncope risk category must not delay its management.

### B — The cause of syncope is certain or highly likely

The paper gives severe aortic stenosis and complete heart block as examples. Follow the appropriate cause-specific pathway. A known cause can be dangerous; diagnostic certainty is not equivalent to discharge suitability.

### C — Syncope is likely, but the cause is uncertain

This is the group for which the next risk-stratification phase is especially useful. Use the episode, background history, examination and ECG to judge the likelihood of an important underlying condition. The absence of a final aetiological diagnosis does not prevent a reasoned decision about observation or discharge.

### D — The diagnosis of syncope itself is doubtful

Reassess for non-syncopal TLOC and other disturbances of consciousness. The article retains this possibility even late in the pathway. No clinical algorithm should convert uncertainty into diagnostic certainty simply because the patient has reached its last box.

## 6 — Stratify risk without confusing it with certainty

Risk stratification concerns a potentially serious underlying condition and the need for further evaluation. The paper adopts the ESC low-, intermediate- and high-risk categories; it does not create a new score or publish a validation study of those categories. Its inputs are the event, medical history, examination and ECG. [1, part 3; pp. 256–257]

| Risk category | What the EUSEM paper says | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Discharge according to local practice, with outpatient care as appropriate. | A complete aetiological work-up need not occur in the ED when the assessment supports low risk. |
| Intermediate | Neither low nor high; further work-up and monitoring, without direct discharge at this point. | Diagnostic uncertainty needs an active plan rather than a reassuring label. |
| High | Further hospital-based care with monitoring and evaluation. | An intermittent serious cause may remain relevant after symptoms have resolved. |

> [!info] ESC context — disposition, not an automatic bed allocation
> ESC recommends discharge for low-risk presentations, early intensive assessment for high risk, and ED/syncope-unit observation for patients who are neither low nor high risk (Class I, level B). High risk can be managed through an appropriate observation or specialist pathway; inpatient admission is favoured by serious coexisting disease, injury, or urgent tests/treatment unavailable through that pathway. [2, §4.1.2, recommendation table, Fig. 6 and Table 7]

The Canvas deliberately preserves the EUSEM article's observation/monitoring route for intermediate risk. It does not turn the broader ESC discussion of selected outpatient assessment into permission for automatic discharge.

## 7 — Make the disposition understandable

The paper's endpoint is a transition of care, not simply an exit door. A useful practical handover or discharge explanation records what was established, what remained uncertain, which risk features mattered and who will complete the work-up. This documentation structure is an educational application of the paper, not a validated discharge checklist. [1, Summary of key results; p. 257]

For a low-risk discharge, document the likely mechanism or remaining uncertainty, relevant negative and positive findings, advice, safety-netting and follow-up. For observation or admission, state the unresolved concern and the question that monitoring or further investigation is intended to answer. Local resources, associated injuries and other care needs still affect what is feasible.

The paper does not establish a universal monitoring duration. This Canvas therefore contains no fixed “observe for X hours, then discharge” rule. Nor does it provide detailed treatment regimens for individual causes. Those decisions belong to the appropriate disease-specific pathway.

## 8 — Two cases to understand the branches

These are fictional teaching examples, not patients from the paper.

**A likely reflex episode.** A patient collapses after prolonged standing, with nausea and warmth beforehand, rapid recovery, no concerning cardiac background and a reassuring evaluation. The initial assessment may support a low-risk reflex mechanism. Discharge becomes a reasoned decision with advice and follow-up, rather than a reward for normal tests.

**An unresolved potentially cardiac event.** A patient with known structural heart disease has an abrupt unexplained blackout. The initial ECG does not capture an arrhythmia. In this example, the unresolved concern warrants monitored further evaluation: the absence of an arrhythmia during one brief recording is not a diagnosis of its absence during the event.

The difference is the whole clinical picture. The first patient's cause may be highly likely; the second patient's cause may still be unknown. Risk and diagnostic certainty are separate dimensions.

## 9 — What the paper establishes, and what it does not

The authors used a modified three-round Delphi process. A core group translated approaches from several European settings into a process model, which was reviewed and revised by the broader group. The result is a consensus structure for care, presented as an extended event process chain (eEPC), plus an example clinical flowchart. [1, Methods and Figs. 1–3; pp. 251–255]

The eEPC is a design framework: it describes actions, decisions, information and transitions so that departments can adapt them to their own staffing and infrastructure. The elaborate original diagram is not intended to be memorised or applied as a rigid bedside script.

The study did **not** prospectively compare this pathway with usual care or demonstrate reductions in missed diagnoses, mortality or admissions. Its limitations include the relatively small expert group, the consensus nature of its conclusions and the work required to translate the model into local practice. Outcome benefits remain a question for further research. [1, Limitations and Conclusions; p. 258]

## 10 — Common reading errors

| Misreading | More faithful interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| “All blackouts are syncope.” | Start with the event and keep non-syncopal explanations open. |
| “Trauma excludes syncope.” | Trauma can cause TLOC or result from it; establish the sequence. |
| “The laboratory table is a mandatory panel.” | Tests depend on the setting and suspected disease. |
| “A normal ECG means low risk.” | A brief trace is one part of the assessment and may miss an intermittent mechanism. |
| “Unexplained means safe.” | Unexplained syncope is precisely where risk assessment is needed. |
| “High risk always means an inpatient bed.” | Urgent monitored evaluation is required; the suitable location depends on needs and resources. |
| “Every cause must be diagnosed before leaving the ED.” | The ED must determine a safe next step; some work-up continues elsewhere. |
| “Expert agreement proves improved outcomes.” | This is a consensus process framework, not an outcome trial. |
